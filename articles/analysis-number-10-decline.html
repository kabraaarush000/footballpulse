The Decline of the Traditional Number 10: Why Football’s Classic Playmaker Had to Evolve

By FootballPulse Analysis • 14 May 2026 • 8 min read

The traditional number 10 was once the most romantic role in football. Positioned between midfield and attack, the number 10 was the artist, the creator, the player expected to find impossible passes and produce magic in crowded spaces. But modern football has changed. Pressing, defensive structure, positional play and physical intensity have made the classic free number 10 harder to fit into elite systems. The role has not completely disappeared, but it has been forced to evolve.

The Classic Number 10

The classic number 10 was given freedom. He often played behind the striker, drifting between the lines and looking for pockets of space. His main job was to create. He did not always need to press aggressively, track full-backs or cover midfield zones. The team accepted this because his creativity could decide matches.

These players were loved because they made football feel unpredictable. A single touch could change the direction of an attack. A disguised pass could break a defensive line. A moment of imagination could turn a slow match into something unforgettable. The number 10 was often the emotional centre of the team.

But the freedom that made the role beautiful also became its weakness. As football became faster and more organised, teams could no longer afford passengers without the ball. Every player had to contribute to the structure. The number 10 could no longer live only in moments of possession.

“The number 10 did not disappear because creativity stopped mattering. It changed because modern football demands creativity with responsibility.”

Pressing Changed Everything

The rise of pressing was one of the biggest reasons the traditional number 10 declined. Modern teams defend from the front. Forwards press centre-backs, wingers lock onto full-backs, and midfielders jump to close central spaces. If one attacking player does not press, the whole system can break.

In older systems, a number 10 could conserve energy and wait for the ball. Today, that is much harder. If the number 10 does not help block the opponent’s defensive midfielder or press the centre-backs, the opponent can build through midfield easily. One weak pressing link creates a route out.

This does not mean creative players cannot exist. It means creative players must now work without the ball. The modern playmaker must understand pressing triggers, close passing lanes and defend with tactical discipline. Talent alone is not enough.

The Midfield Became More Crowded

Another reason the traditional number 10 struggled is that central spaces became more crowded. Teams now defend compactly, with midfielders and defenders positioned close together. The space between the lines is smaller than before. A player receiving there is immediately surrounded.

This makes life difficult for a playmaker who depends on time and space. In modern football, receiving between the lines requires quicker scanning, stronger physical resistance and faster decision-making. Opponents do not give the number 10 the luxury of turning freely.

Because of this, many creative players moved into different roles. Some became wide playmakers, starting from the wing and drifting inside. Others became advanced number 8s, contributing to midfield pressing while still creating chances. Some became false 9s, dropping from the striker position to link play.

The Rise of the Number 8

As the traditional number 10 declined, the advanced number 8 became more important. This role allows a creative player to start slightly deeper, participate in midfield structure and still arrive in attacking areas. The number 8 has more responsibility but also more involvement.

A modern number 8 must press, carry the ball, combine with wide players and arrive in the box. This role suits many creative players because it gives them space to influence the game without being isolated between defenders. They can receive facing forward more often and connect the team from deeper areas.

The number 8 also fits pressing systems better. In a midfield three, the advanced midfielders can jump forward to press while still having a defensive midfielder behind them. This gives the team balance. The creative player is not removed from defensive responsibility, but he is supported by the structure.

Wide Playmakers Replaced Central Playmakers

Many modern creators now begin from wide positions. This gives them more room to receive and attack. Instead of starting in the crowded central zone, they start near the touchline and move inside when the moment is right. This is why inverted wingers have become so important.

A wide playmaker can receive the ball with more space, face the defender and choose whether to dribble, pass inside or combine with an overlapping full-back. This role still gives the player creative freedom, but it is easier to fit into a team shape because the winger also has clear defensive duties.

The wide role also creates better angles. A left-footed player on the right side, or a right-footed player on the left side, can move inside and look for shots or through balls. This has become one of the most common ways teams replace the classic central number 10.

The False 9 as a Creative Solution

Another evolution of the number 10 idea is the false 9. Instead of playing behind a striker, the creative player becomes the striker on paper but drops into midfield to link play. This movement can confuse defenders. If a centre-back follows him, space opens behind. If the defender stays, the false 9 can receive between the lines.

The false 9 role works best when the team has runners around him. Wide forwards must attack the spaces he creates. Midfielders must support. Without movement around him, the false 9 can drop deep and leave nobody threatening the goal.

This role shows that playmaking has not disappeared. It has moved. The creative player no longer has to wear the number 10 position in the old way. Creativity can come from the wing, midfield, fullback zones or even the striker position.

Defensive Demands Are Higher

Modern football demands defensive contribution from everyone. Even the most creative players must press, block passing lanes and understand the team’s shape. A player who creates chances but leaves huge spaces without the ball can become a tactical problem.

This is especially true in big matches. Against elite opponents, one free player can control the game. If the number 10 does not help defensively, the opposition defensive midfielder may receive comfortably and dictate play. Managers are less willing to accept that risk.

The modern creative player must therefore become more complete. He must still provide imagination, but he must also be physically prepared and tactically disciplined. The beauty of the role remains, but the responsibilities have multiplied.

Creativity Has Become Collective

In older football, one number 10 was often the main source of creativity. Today, creativity is more collective. Centre-backs progress the ball, fullbacks invert into midfield, number 8s arrive between the lines, wingers create from wide areas and strikers link play. Chance creation is spread across the team.

This makes teams less dependent on one player. If the opponent marks one creator, another can step forward. The system creates opportunities through movement and spacing, not only individual genius. This is why modern managers often prefer flexible creators instead of one free playmaker.

However, this does not mean individual brilliance is gone. It simply operates inside a more structured environment. The best modern creators are still capable of magic, but their magic is supported by tactical order.

Can the Traditional Number 10 Return?

Football always moves in cycles. Roles disappear, evolve and sometimes return in new forms. The traditional number 10 may not return exactly as before, but there will always be space for players who can receive between the lines and create danger. The difference is that these players must now offer more.

A modern number 10 can still exist if he presses intelligently, moves flexibly and contributes to the team shape. He may not have complete freedom, but he can still be the creative heartbeat. The role is not dead. It has become more demanding.

The future may belong to hybrid players: part number 10, part number 8, part winger, part presser. These players will carry the spirit of the old playmaker while fitting the needs of modern football.

Conclusion

The decline of the traditional number 10 is not the death of creativity. It is the evolution of creativity. Modern football still needs players who see passes others do not, unlock defences and change matches with imagination. But those players must now work within systems that demand pressing, movement and defensive responsibility.

The classic free playmaker belonged to a different tactical era. Today’s game is faster, more compact and more collective. The number 10 has not vanished; it has transformed. The magic is still there, but now it must run, press, rotate and defend too.
